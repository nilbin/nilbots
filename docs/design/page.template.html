nilbots · redesign study · web + mobile chrome · renderers untouched

Six nav items, four tabs, and nowhere does it say what you came to do.

The chrome today is a set of destinations — Arena, Bots, Leaderboard, Store, Docs, My garage — six different kinds of thing given equal weight in one row. Mobile keeps four and silently drops the rest.

Nine directions follow: three worked through in depth, five shorter ones, and a ninth that fuses two of them. Every one changes navigation and page composition, not just palette. Chassis art is the real sprite throughout — it is the strongest identifier the product owns, and it belongs next to a bot's name everywhere.

The loop, and who owns each stage

write→submit→fight→read it back→decide what to change

The first two belong to the editor and nilbots submit and should stay there. The server runs the fight. These surfaces own one stage — reading a match back — and not the one after it. No direction below moves building into a browser.

Worked through

Nav, home screen, phone screen, type scale and density.

Ladder

the product is a competition

Standing is the spine. Home is the ladder itself with your row pinned wherever you scroll, and every match is reached from a row as evidence of how a rank was earned. Recent form sits inline, so the table answers “who is climbing” without a second page.

  • Six nav items become four; the store folds into a bot's appearance and docs becomes a utility link.
  • The arena feed stops being a destination — it becomes the per-row form strip and a “just finished” rail.
  • Mobile gets the same four, so the surfaces stop disagreeing.
  • Rank is the display type: large tabular numerals carry hierarchy instead of headings.

Readout

the half of the loop the CLI cannot show you

You build in the CLI, and this does not argue with that. It designs the other half: what you open after nilbots submit to find out whether the change worked. Home is the current generation's readout — what is live, what it changed against the generation before, and what the rating did about it. That last part exists nowhere today, in the CLI or here.

  • Nav is the readback, not the build: Bot · Results · Compare · Docs.
  • Home pairs the diff between submitted generations with the rating delta over the games since.
  • Light ground, mono promoted to display, so it matches the terminal beside it rather than the arena it reports on.

Broadcast

the product is the match

The thing worth showing someone is a fight. Home leads with one featured match at full width — live if anything is live, otherwise the best recent one — and everything else is a rail beneath it. The ladder becomes context you can open, not the spine.

  • Home is a feature plus rails: the shape of a schedule, not a table.
  • Matches get titles, with map and rules as a byline.
  • Warm ground from ember-forge, one cold teal reserved for what is live right now.

It is the only direction that gives a reason to come back on a day you did not submit anything.

Counter

the product is the matchup

Rating tells you that you lost; it never tells you to whom, repeatedly. Home is the matrix: your bot against the ladder, cell by cell, so a losing streak resolves into “Warden beats you 7 times in 9 and nobody else does”. That is the most actionable thing this product could show a person who writes bots.

The two you pointed at, put together

Ladder gives the season stakes; the season gives the ladder a memory. Worked through as one direction rather than two features bolted together, because the fusion forces a decision neither made alone.

Climb

standing, with a time axis

A ladder alone is a snapshot: it tells you where you are and nothing about whether you are going anywhere. A season alone is a story with no stakes. Together, the table is the season — every row carries where it started, where it is now and the shape of the journey between, so “who is climbing” is legible without opening anything.

  • Each row gains two columns that only exist because of the season: movement since it opened, and a sparkline of that bot's rating across it.
  • Your row expands in place into the full trajectory, with your submissions marked on it — the season chart is not a separate screen.
  • The season frame gives the ladder a deadline, which is what makes a rank worth defending.
  • Rows sort by rating but can re-sort by movement, which turns the same table into “who is hot right now”.

How they differ, in one line each

  • Ladder — for players who already have a bot; smallest build, best density, nothing for newcomers.
  • Readout — the only one that answers “did my last change help”; most work, and the one thing the CLI structurally cannot do.
  • Broadcast — for people who will never write a bot; best for sharing a link, worst mid-loop.
  • Rack — puts the chassis art at the size it deserves and makes the store the obvious empty slot rather than a separate errand.
  • Field guide — the only one that helps someone decide what to build; needs real matchup analysis behind it or it is decoration.
  • Terminal — the most coherent with how people actually build, and the least welcoming to anyone who has not started.
  • Season — the only one giving a reason to return on a day you did not submit; depends on seasons existing first.
  • Counter — the most actionable screen in the set, and the data is already in the database.
  • Climb — Ladder and Season as one thing: the table becomes the season, and your trajectory lives inside your own row rather than on a page of its own.

The identity chip, state pill and tabular treatment are the same in all nine and would land as shared primitives whichever wins. Two of these — Counter and Readout — are the only ones that show something the product does not currently know how to say; the other six rearrange what is already there.
