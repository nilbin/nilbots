nilbots · climb · specification

The table is the season.

A ladder alone is a snapshot — it says where you are and nothing about whether you are going anywhere. A season alone is a story without stakes. Climb puts the time axis inside the standings: every row carries where it opened, where it is now, and the shape of the journey between.

What follows is the part that needs deciding before any of it gets built: the logotype, what colour is allowed to mean, and how the screen behaves when a player has six bots instead of one.

Correction

Colour belongs to players, not to the system

I previously picked magenta for the season layer on the grounds that it was 77° from the nearest chassis trim. That analysis measured the wrong set. Accent is not derived from the chassis — AppearanceEditor gives every bot an <input type="color">, so a player picks the look and the accent independently and can choose any hex, including that magenta.

There is therefore no safe hue to reserve, and the rule has to come from the other direction.

Chroma means somebody chose it. Player accents are the only saturated colour on the screen. Everything the system says — the season, the deadline, the qualifying band, structure, rules — is achromatic and expressed as form: a dashed rule, a sparkline's shape, a weight change. The two cannot collide because they are not competing on the same channel.

Outcome keeps one exception. A rise and a fall carry a green or red glyph, never a fill or a row tint, so they read at a glance without ever sitting behind a player's colour. Every accent still passes through adjustAccentForBackground before it is drawn, which the codebase already does — a player who picks near-black is still legible on a near-black field.

One consequence worth noticing: the identity chip now shows both choices — the chassis inside a ring of the accent. Today the accent is a small dot beside a name, which wastes the more distinctive of the two picks.

Ground

Forge — the arena is a machine shop, so the chrome is the shop

The palette is not invented. It is arena-01's own map theme: ember-forge/theme.json gives canvas #090504, frame #684229 and zone #38d9c5, and those three become the field, the structure and the rise colour. The chrome is made of the material the game is made of, which is the whole argument for it.

Burnt orange #c8763c is a material, not a signal. It is in the ground, the hairline under the top bar and the wordmark — places that mean nothing. It never marks state, never marks “yours”, never marks a bot. The policy above is unchanged: the only chroma that means something is a player's accent.

Warm accents lose their edge. Murder Roomba's #f5a623 sits near the ground's own hue, so on a warm panel it starts reading as furniture. Two things answer it: adjustAccentForBackground already exists and simply has more work to do here, and the identity chip now keeps a near-black disc behind the ring so a warm accent always has a cold field to sit on. A player who picks something close to #c8763c is still the hard case, and no adjustment makes their bot feel like a choice on this ground.

Rise and fall had to move. Rise is now #38d9c5, the theme's own zone teal, which ties it to the arena instead of being a generic green. Fall was #ff6b62 — close enough to the ground's family that it stopped reading as an alarm — and moves to #f4526b, pushed toward rose so it separates. Player accents are untouched, so Bastille's #ef4444 is exactly what its owner picked. That means a player's red and the system's fall glyph now sit close together, which is the sharpest argument against Forge and worth seeing before you commit to it.

One more thing that does not change: the arena themes. bastion-01 is control-room, which is cold blue; gallery-01 is frost-relay, which is nearly white. Warm chrome around a cold arena is the common case rather than the exception — the viewer further down is exactly that, and it is the honest test.

Logotype

Path, wall, floor — one set of tiles, three ways

The current mark sets two spans and hard-codes #38bdf8 on the word “bots”, which breaks the moment the accent belongs to a player and disappears entirely in one-colour contexts — a favicon, a CLI banner, an embroidered patch. The replacement is not drawn. It is constructed on the arena's own grid.

Letters are polylines through tile centres, right angles only, because that is how a bot moves. Rasterise the polyline and you have the tiles; trace the tiles and you have the outline; round every corner — convex and concave alike, at the radius wallSolids.ts chamfers walls with — and the letterform is finished. The payoff is that one cell set renders as the arena's three primitives: the trace is the route, the matter is the wall, and the plan is the floor.

Matter is the wordmark, the plan mark is the icon, and the trace is what the wordmark does while a season loads. The construction, the alphabet and the size tests are on their own page.

Type

One family, three cuts, and one machine voice

The screen was nearly all monospace: navigation, buttons, pills, the whole table body, legends, captions, and even running prose. Mono carries a meaning — a machine wrote this — and when everything is mono, nothing is. It also costs real room: a ladder set in a fixed-width face spends horizontal space that long bot names need.

Archivo has a width axis, so one variable file gives three voices — condensed for labels, normal for reading, expanded for display. That is the same economy as one cell set with three finishes, and it leaves mono with exactly one job.

Several bots

A player is a stable, not a competitor

A player owns many bots at once, so “your standing” is not one number. Climb answers that in three places: a fleet strip that shows every bot's position at once, one chart with a line per bot in that bot's own accent, and a ladder that marks every row of yours rather than pinning a single favourite.

This is where player-picked accents earn their keep. Six overlaid trajectories are unreadable in one colour and instantly separable in six — and because the grid, the axis and the labels are achromatic, the only thing colour distinguishes is which bot is which.

The top bar does not wrap. The frame below is a desktop screen, so on a phone it scrolls sideways inside its own container rather than reflowing into a shape the product would never render — which is what the mock was doing, and it is the one wrong answer, because it changes the height of the chrome as the window resizes. Below 640px the real rule is to drop the tab row from the top bar entirely and use the bottom tab bar the phone already has; the top bar keeps the wordmark, the season, and nothing else.

What this needs before it can be built

Seasons do not exist yet. Everything else here is already in the database — ratings over time, rank movement, per-bot history, several bots per player. The season boundary, its length, and whether the top of the table carries any consequence are product decisions, and the qualifying band shown above is a placeholder I invented to demonstrate the form.

Movement needs a rank snapshot. “▲14 places” requires the rank at season open, which nothing records today. It is one column on a table, but it has to start being written before the first season can report it.

2.5D becomes the default. Decided — but the change is “default to the dimensional renderer where it exists”, not “default to it”. Three things follow. The CLI's single-file artifact stubs three.js out entirely in vite.cli.config.ts, so DIMENSIONAL_RENDERER_AVAILABLE is false there and that build must stay flat. The golden-frame tests hash the Canvas2D canvas, so the harness has to be pinned to the flat renderer in the same commit or the regression guard turns itself off silently. And the viewer is on the CLI compatibility surface: CliVersion gets bumped and publish-cli runs before publish-and-deploy, or assert-cli-release.sh refuses the deploy.

Dropping the mobile WebView: yes, and almost all of the renderer is shared. The prize is the hand-mirrored bridge in mobile/src/components/arena/protocol.ts, HostedViewer and the whole WebView lifecycle. The DOM is not woven through the renderer — it sits in three seams at the edges, and the measurements are below rather than an impression. Of 3,244 lines in render3d/, about 2,520 are already platform-neutral, and the 2,100 lines it imports — replayModel, replayPresentation, interpolate, wallTopology, replayParticipants — contain zero DOM references today.

  • Textures inrender/arenaThemes.ts loads the atlases (10 DOM references) and two signatures type them as HTMLImageElement. Take a THREE.Texture instead: new Image() on web, expo-asset + expo-three's loadAsync on native.
  • Textures made181 lines in five canvas factories — progressRing, radialGlow, tracerTexture, spriteTexture, flare. Two depend on a player's accent so they cannot simply be baked, but they are a gradient and a distance field: a DataTexture or a short shader replaces both.
  • Geometry derivedchassisModel.ts, 312 lines, all SVGLoader and DOMParser. Deterministic, so it moves to build time and ships geometry JSON — which also deletes SVGLoader from the web bundle and takes SVG parsing out of the viewer's cold start. A win on both platforms rather than a tax mobile pays alone.
  • Written twiceArenaCanvas3D.tsx, 227 lines: canvas, WebGLRenderer, ResizeObserver, raycaster. Native gets a <GLView> of the same shape. This one should be written twice — it is the part that is the platform.

Two consequences beyond the arithmetic. Extracting wallTopology into the shared package forces a dedup already owed — the flat renderer re-inlined its own copy and it was never re-extracted. And the Canvas2D renderer cannot come along; it is CanvasRenderingContext2D. Native means mobile has only the 3D path and loses what onUnavailable falls back to, which is consistent with making 2.5D the default but is real exposure on a weak Android GPU — and issue #16's fullscreen lag says the budget is not generous today.

The renderer toggle needs a name and a home. “2.5D” is a dimension count, not something a player recognizes, and a header button is the wrong place for a preference that should be remembered. Naming it is a product decision I did not want to make on your behalf.

The timeline needs no new data. Every mark in it — fires, hits, destructions, with actor and tick — is already in the replay's causal events; the feed is rendering exactly those today, just as a flat list. What it does need is a decision about which kinds earn a mark once a Frontline match has six lives instead of two, or the lanes stop being readable.

The first-run screen assumes an installer that may not exist. brew install nilbots and nilbots new are written as though they ship; check both before this goes near a build, because the whole screen's value is that every command on it works when typed.

The logotype is a component, not an asset. It replaces web/src/components/Logo.tsx and its mobile counterpart, and the favicon in index.html — all three currently hard-code #38bdf8, which is the accent this design stops treating as a brand colour. Each becomes one <svg> holding a single path: under a kilobyte, no font dependency, and correct in both themes without a second file.
